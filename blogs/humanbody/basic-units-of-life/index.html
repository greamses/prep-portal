Tiny Builders: Cells

Did you know your body is made of millions of tiny factories called cells? What would happen if you could see them buzzing inside you?

Prep Portal Science Desk· cells · life · structure· Grade 4
Quick Facts
Size
A typical cell is only about 10 micrometres across – about the width of a human hair.
Number
Your body has roughly 37 trillion cells, more than the number of grains of sand on many beaches.
Parts
Each cell has a membrane, cytoplasm and a nucleus that holds DNA.
Meet the Cell

Cells are tiny building blocks that make up every part of you, from the skin on your arm to the brain that solves maths. In Nigeria, the cells in your hand are the same as those in a Lagos market vendor’s hand. They look like tiny bubbles under a microscope and each has a thin outer wall called a membrane. Cells are unique because they can grow, make energy, and pass messages. Highlight terms: cell membrane and nucleus.

"

One single cell can make enough energy to power a tiny LED light for a few seconds.

How it works

Think of a cell like a small factory. The nucleus is the boss that stores the instructions (DNA) for everything. The mitochondria are power stations that turn food into energy, just like a generator on a danfo bus. The cell membrane acts like a security gate, letting nutrients in and waste out, similar to a school gate that lets students enter but keeps strangers out. Inside, the cytoplasm is the floor where workers (proteins) move around doing jobs. All together, cells grow, repair, and send signals, keeping you alive.

  • Nucleus - holds the DNA blueprint.
  • Mitochondria - makes energy from sugar.
  • Cell membrane - controls what enters and leaves.
Why it matters

Without cells you could not grow taller, run a football match, or even think about jollof rice. Cells repair cuts, fight germs, and give you the strength to carry your school bag. They also help your brain remember the multiplication tables you learned. In short, cells are the reason you can play, learn, and celebrate festivals.

  1. Step 1 - Nutrition.
    Food you eat is broken down into sugar that enters cells for energy.
  2. Step 2 - Growth.
    Cells use the energy to build new parts, letting you grow taller each year.
  3. Step 3 - Repair.
    When you get a cut, cells multiply to close the wound.
"

Some cells can live for many years, like the nerve cells that help you hear your favourite music.

Try This

Make a Cell Model. Use a clear plastic bottle for the membrane, a small marble for the nucleus, and colored beads for mitochondria. Fill the bottle with water, drop the marble and beads inside, and watch how the “cell” looks under a lamp.
